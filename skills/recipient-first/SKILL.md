# recipient-first

Picture the recipient before you write: what they already know, what they are
busy with, what saying yes costs them, and what is in it for them. Write from
there. Most good messaging falls out of that one habit. The rest of this skill
covers the places where that picture predictably goes wrong, and one line you
never cross.

## Where the picture goes wrong

- **You can't un-know what you know.** Your draft is clearer to you than to
  them. Name what you assumed they already knew.
- **You underestimate how often people say yes**, by about half. Ask, and ask
  directly. A hint is not a request.
- **Tone doesn't survive text.** If irony or mild criticism carries meaning, say
  it literally.
- **You argue from your own values.** Make the case in terms they already hold.

## Subtract first

People reach for additions and overlook removals. When revising, first ask
what can go.

- Cut jargon, preamble, restating, hedges you don't mean, and secondary asks.
- Cut work from them: attach the file, propose two times, send the draft
  instead of asking for one.
- Add only what they can't act without: missing context, the real cost, the
  real deadline.

## Shape

- One ask, one owner, a concrete action: who does what, by when.
- Uncertainty as a number ("3–5 days", "about 70%"), not a vague word. "I think
  X" is fine; "X, maybe, sort of" costs credibility.
- Simple is not casual. Keep the register the relationship expects.
- Bad news first, with the required action attached to it.
- Apology: own the responsibility, then offer a concrete repair. Where there is
  legal or liability exposure, leave the responsibility sentence as a
  placeholder for whoever owns it.
- Disagreement: restate their view in a form they'd accept before giving yours.
  This protects the relationship; don't expect it to move their position.
- One concrete case beats a general claim.
- For an important, refusable ask, in person beats text when the channel is
  yours to choose. If it isn't, write for the channel you were given.

## Honesty

- **Test:** would they still call the ask fair if they knew exactly how the
  message was built? Real deadlines, real exclusivity, real costs are in.
  Invented ones are out.
- No manufactured urgency or scarcity, no repeating a claim to make it feel
  true, no "thanks in advance" that books the yes before they give it.
- Don't invent their motives or the facts. Ask. If you can't, draft anyway and
  state your assumptions in one line above the draft.
- If the user asks for a manipulative move, write the honest version and say in
  one line what you left out and why.

## Output

- The draft. Above it, a few lines on the recipient's state and any
  assumptions, when the message is more than routine.
- Skip that analysis for short routine messages, but never for apologies, bad
  news, or anything crossing a power gap.
- If the existing draft is already good, say so and change nothing.

## Limits

- Not for layout or scannability (see `readable-message`), marketing, cold
  outreach, or adversarial counterparties such as legal disputes.
- The research behind this is mostly English-language. In languages with
  grammaticalized politeness, such as Japanese, judge the register locally.
