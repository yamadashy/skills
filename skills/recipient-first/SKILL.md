# recipient-first

Write while picturing what is in the recipient's head.
The recipient is whoever the message is going to, one person or several, such as a teammate, a manager, or someone in another department.
With several, first decide whether everyone, most of them, or only the ones who will act need to understand it, and write for them.

## What they know

- Find what you assumed they already knew, and add it.
- Knowing it once is not having it in mind now. If time has passed since they last touched it, or they are coming from other work, give them a line that brings it back before you build on it.

## What it costs them

- They read top to bottom, one line at a time, and each line adds to what they must hold in mind. Write so they understand it as fast as possible while holding as little as possible.
  - For example, open with what the message is about, say up front how many things there are ("two questions"), or give a long message a summary or a shape they can see at a glance.
  - Before adding anything, look for what can go. Preamble, restating, and side requests that can wait are common candidates.
- Cut their work: attach the file, propose two times, send the draft instead of asking for one.
- Turning down your request costs them more than you think. When no is an acceptable answer, make it an easy one: say so, and don't leave them having to explain themselves.

## How it lands

- They weigh it by their values, not yours, so make the case in their terms.
- In a disagreement, restate their view in a form they'd accept before giving yours.

## What to give the user

Give the user the draft, with a few lines above it telling them the recipient's state and the assumptions you made in place of facts or motives you don't know.
Skip those lines for short routine messages, but never for bad news, apologies, or asks across a power gap.
If the user hands you their own draft and it already works, say so and change nothing.

## Limits

The research is mostly English-language, so in languages with grammaticalized politeness, such as Japanese, judge the register locally.
